Compaction, decay and reinforcement in a two-tier conversational memory: a paired evaluation against retrieval-augmented generation
双层对话记忆中的压缩、衰减与强化：与检索增强生成的配对评估
A conversational agent that remembers has to decide what to keep. Retrieval-augmented generation keeps the transcript and searches it; the alternative is to compact each turn into atomic facts and search those. We compare the two on LoCoMo, with both arms answering all 1,985 questions in one window, against the same stores, with the same generator and judge, and both predictions on one row, which licenses McNemar's exact test on the 512 questions where they disagree. On the four categories comparable work reports, compaction scores 71.4 per cent against 64.6, a lead of 6.8 points (p = 5.5e-08) at 34 per cent fewer prompt tokens. On all five, including the adversarial set comparable work drops, the lead falls to 2.0 and stops being significant, because the baseline leads adversarial by 14.6. That loss has a structure. Our lead falls as the reference answer demands more source wording, 9.6 points ahead at one or two reference words and 3.1 behind past eight, with token overlap moving the same way, so the judge is not the cause. Compaction keeps what a fact asserts and discards how it was said, so it wins where the answer is a value and loses where it is a phrasing. We also report four negative results with their power calculations.

Large language model (LLM) agents coordinate through conversation, and the same message can be phrased in simpler or more complex language, bluntly or politely. We test whether such differences change how other agents judge and act, using the LURK Werewolf framework, which separates each utterance's speech-act predicate from its natural-language realization. We rewrite only the realization, check that the game content is preserved, and replay each game day from saved checkpoints against a re-sampled control. Steerable Multi-Objective Group-Relative Preference Optimization with Jointly Trained Low-Rank Adapters
基于联合训练低秩适配器的可调控多目标组相对偏好优化
Language model deployment often requires different trade-offs between competing objectives as user preferences change, yet standard post-training methods typically produce a single policy for a fixed objective weighting. We introduce LambdaMix, a new parameter efficient method for learning a preference conditioned family of policies with group-relative online reinforcement learning. LambdaMix jointly trains one low-rank adapter per objective over a frozen base model. One preference vector selects the adapter mixture that generates responses and weights the separately normalized rewards used to update the adapters. A single training run supports changing preferences at inference without further optimization. We experiment with four models on helpfulness and harmlessness and on solving math problems while following instructions. We also evaluate the two largest models on rewriting comments to preserve wording while reducing clues about personal attributes. On the first two tasks, LambdaMix achieves higher hypervolume than all evaluated conditioned and policy merging baselines across all four models. LambdaMix uses fewer training completions and stores fewer adapter parameters than a set of GRPO specialist policies. With architecture and initialization fixed, varying adapter mixtures and objective weights together gives wider score ranges and higher coverage than varying either alone.

RuACT: Recovering Inactive Rubrics as Adaptive Critics and Teachers
RuACT：恢复失效评分标准作为自适应批评者与教师
Rubric-based reinforcement learning (RL) extends RL with verifiable rewards to open-ended tasks by scoring each response against query-specific rubrics. Under group-relative optimization, however, a rubric that every sampled response fails (unreachable) or passes (saturated) gives all responses the same outcome and no learning signal; in Qwen3-4B medical training, 60.88% of query-rubric observations are inactive. We find that the two states lack different supervision: stating an unreachable rubric in the prompt raises its satisfaction from 1.13% to 91.25%, whereas responses that all pass a saturated rubric still differ in quality. We therefore propose RuACT (Rubrics as Adaptive Critics and Teachers), which assigns each inactive rubric the role that supplies what it lacks. Rubric as Teacher uses unreachable rubrics as privileged information for reward-gated on-policy self-distillation, and Rubric as Critic decomposes saturated rubrics into child rubrics that separate current responses while sharing the parent's weight. RuACT outperforms GRPO, rubric reweighting, and rubric-guided exploration on all four medical and scientific benchmarks with Qwen3-4B and achieves the best average with Qwen3-1.7B. On initially unreachable rubrics, unguided recovery rises from 35.02% to 42.86%, while child refinement improves reward-quality alignment on independent rollouts from 57.74% to 60.77%. Code is available at https://github.com/ZJU-REAL/RuACT.

Benchmarking Speaker Embedders in the Sub-Second Regime for Real-Time Voice AI Agents
面向实时语音AI智能体的亚秒级说话人嵌入器基准测试
Real-time Voice AI agents must separate the active user from intelligible background speech that survives conventional noise suppression. Background sources include television audio, family members, and colleagues. Production denoisers remove non-stationary environmental noise effectively. Background speech shares the spectral characteristics of the target and passes through, causing false acceptances in downstream speaker verification (SV) and automatic speech recognition (ASR). We benchmark open-source speaker embedders TitaNet-Large, TitaNet-Small, ECAPA-TDNN, CAM++, and SpeakerNet under a strict sub-second latency budget. Evaluation combines a controlled benchmark with a weighted human-annotated production set. Our controlled benchmark is built from real calls in which a second real speaker is injected into silence intervals at modulated amplitude. We report three findings. First, denoising before embedding is generally harmful and raises equal error rate (EER) across architectures. Second, truncating audio below the full voice-activity segment degrades every model. Third, roughly half of the TitaNet embedding is redundant for this task. Trailing dimensions encode population attributes such as language and gender. Such attributes carry little discriminative value in a demographically homogeneous user base. We distill these results into deployment guidance. Verification should run on raw audio with full voice-activity chunks. Thresholds should favor precision over F1. Truncated embeddings reduce the cost of similarity search.

Our analysis of existing Korean benchmarks reveals that most questions can be answered through factual recall alone, while those demanding greater application also differ in required knowledge, making it difficult to isolate application ability. To address this, we introduce KLEAR, a framework that constructs knowledge-matched contrasts by varying application demand while preserving the required knowledge. During construction, KLEAR identifies the knowledge underlying each source question as an explicit anchor bundle to guide generation of questions requiring more complex application. Generated candidates then undergo automated and human validation, with iterative revision based on evaluation feedback. Experiments demonstrate that the resulting knowledge-matched sets largely satisfy the intended constraints, enabling controlled evaluation across diverse LLMs. These evaluations reveal that increasing application demand exposes failures that persist even when the required knowledge is provided.

Benchmarks for open-ended generation often rely on large language model (LLM) judges, whose reliability in low-resource languages is uncertain. We introduce Khmer PFGen-Bench, to our knowledge the first judge-free benchmark for multi-sentence answers to questions composed directly in Khmer, a low-resource language. Khmer PFGen-Bench adapts the scorer of PFGen-Bench, a benchmark for Japanese that scores responses with keyword rules and n-gram statistics from LLM-generated references. We find that post-trained models can give more valid but less varied references than their base variants, and that mixing model families increases variety for the same number of references. These findings offer guidance for building judge-free benchmarks in other low-resource languages.

PhoPunct: A Large-Scale Benchmark of Transformer Architectures for Vietnamese Punctuation Restoration
PhoPunct：面向越南语标点恢复的大型基准测试
Vietnamese punctuation restoration is often evaluated on relatively small benchmarks, limiting conclusions about how architectural choices scale with data volume. We present a controlled benchmark on News and Novels corpora containing 22.1 million labeled tokens (19.9M News and 2.2M Novels). We evaluate 17 architectures formed from four pretrained Transformer backbones and optional bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) and conditional random field (CRF) layers, together with a PhoBERT-large-Bi-LSTM-CRF model, PhoPunct. Every model is trained for eight epochs with the same learning rate and without architecture-specific tuning. Under this fixed budget, adding Bi-LSTM and CRF layers does not reliably improve macro F1: in seven of eight matched domain-backbone comparisons, a plain Transformer classifier matches or outperforms its Bi-LSTM-CRF counterpart. PhoPunct obtains the best News result (61.35), whereas XLM-R with a CRF obtains the best Novels result (50.18), exceeding PhoPunct (48.97). These results show that the value of recurrent and structured-prediction heads depends on their optimization regime and motivate explicit separation between architectural improvements and improvements due to architecture-specific tuning. Our source code is available at: https://github.com/aisia-research-lab/PhoPunct

AlienBench: A Benchmark of Structural Novelty in Language Model Outputs
AlienBench：语言模型输出中结构新颖性的基准测试
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for creative tasks such as writing, design, and idea generation. For these uses, it matters whether a model's ideas depart from familiar examples or stay close to them. Novelty is one standard measure of creativity, and current creativity evaluations ask human experts or LLM judges to rate how novel an output is. However, these ratings have no fixed reference for what counts as novel (or, in other terms, departure from the familiar). Measuring this departure requires a task with known familiar defaults. The structured imagination task from cognitive psychology is such a task. In this creativity test, people imagine a creature from another planet. The departure from features of Earth animals, such as bilateral symmetry and legs as mode of locomotion, is used as measure of creativity. We introduce AlienBench, a benchmark that adapts the structured imagination task to language models. Models describe alien creatures, and a panel of three LLM judges extracts ten biological features from the description and labels them as either Earth-typical or a departure. The sum of feature departures is the score of a description, from 0 to 10. Each model describes alien creatures under six prompt conditions that vary the framing of the task and the planetary environment. Each model generates 30 descriptions per condition (180 in total), and its overall score is the mean across these descriptions. We evaluate 10 language models, from frontier to open-weight models. Overall scores range from 3.28 (GPT-6 Luna) to 6.55 (Ministral 3 14B). Interestingly, open-weight models score higher than proprietary models in this task (5.40 vs. 4.58). Agent skills are short documents that teach a language model how to handle a class of tasks. To save context, these documents are increasingly compiled into low-rank adapters (LoRA), which keep the behaviour but lose the text: an adapter is hard to read, edit, audit or move to another model. We ask whether the skill document can be written back from the adapter and its frozen base model, and we score each recovered document by the share of the original's key instructions it states and by the tasks it solves. Reading the weights alone recovered little with the methods we tried; a learned map from weights to text passed on about one bit of the document. Running the adapter worked much better. The Executing Writer, a small writer trained on top of the adapted model, recovers 39-44% of the key instructions of held-out skills, against 1.3-1.5% with another skill's adapter, and on adapters fitted to 41 external skills its documents restore 85-91% of the original's task gain. These strongest results are on adapters whose training stored the document; adapters trained on behaviour alone are much harder for trained writers (1-2% of instructions). For these adapters we test an agent that runs the base and adapted models and writes a skill from where they differ. On ALFWorld its skills recover 58-71% of a LoRA's gain and carry over to models that cannot load the LoRA, lifting Llama-3.1-8B from 5% to 55%. Whether such a skill comes from the adapter depends on what the adapter learned: when its behaviour cannot be guessed, the adapter is the source; when it can, a reward loop without the adapter does as well.

We extend LLM-guided reward design, the framework of Qu et al. (2025) in which an LLM evolves the reward of a two-step REINFORCE fraud-blocking policy, to attack this reject-inference problem inside the reward. A single LLM designs a dual-stream reward: stream 1 applies the standard dollar-weighted precision objective to labeled transactions; stream 2 scores legacy-blocked, unlabeled transactions by contrasting the newest gradient-boosted risk signal against the legacy scores that drove the original block. On Seeding Watermarks to Detect Verbatim LLM Copy-Paste Responses
关于在输入端嵌入水印以检测大语言模型原文复制回复的研究
Large language models (LLMs) have made fluent essay writing, code drafting, and quiz answering instantly available to students at every level, from secondary school through graduate study. Many educators do not object to LLM use \emph{per~se}; what they need to detect is the case in which a student pastes the assignment prompt into a chatbot and submits the model's reply verbatim, without engaging with the work. Existing post-hoc AI-text detectors remain unreliable and have been shown to penalise non-native English writers, while output-side watermarks require cooperation from the model provider. We propose an alternative that the educator controls directly: an input-side watermark in which an invisible instruction is embedded inside the visible assignment prompt itself. An LLM that ingests the prompt verbatim quietly reads the hidden instruction and writes a tell-tale signature into its reply, exposing the copy-and-paste pathway specifically. We describe SteganoPrompt, a single-page, zero-dependency web tool that encodes an arbitrary printable-ASCII payload into the deprecated Unicode Tags block (\texttt{U+E0000}--\texttt{U+E007F}). The encoded string is visually identical to the original, survives common copy-paste channels (Word, Google Docs, PDF, Markdown, Slack, e-mail, the major learning-management systems), and is tokenized as ordinary text by most frontier models. We evaluate compliance across eight LLM families and a representative set of educational content channels. To keep deployment transparent, disclosure is built into the tool itself: every encoded brief also carries a brief, always-on academic-integrity reminder for the student, and we set out concrete guidance recommending for its disclosed, ethical use in the classroom. The tool is released under the MIT licence at https://ezharjan.github.io/SteganoPrompt/.
